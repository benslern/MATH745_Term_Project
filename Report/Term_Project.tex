\textbf{Project Proposal}

\noindent
For my term project, I will compute and analyse solutions of the Generalized Constantine-Lax-Majda equation. The Constantine-Lax-Majda (CLM) equation was proposed as a one-dimensional model for the three-dimensional vorticity equation and has the form\vspace{-12pt}
$$\wt - \ux\w = 0, \qquad \ux = H\w\vspace{-12pt}$$
where $\w$ is the vorticity of the fluid and $u$ is the fluid velocity. For this analysis the equations will be examined on the periodic domain $x\in[-\pi,\pi]$. The Hilbert transform of the vorticity is defined as
$$H\w(x,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \w(y,t)\cot\lr\frac{x-y}{t}\rr \,dy,$$
which has a singularity at $x=y$ and so must be evaluated using the Cauchy Principle Value. The CLM equation was later expanded on by De Gregorio to include the convection term $u\wx$. The De Gregorio equation has the form\vspace{-12pt}
$$\wt + u\wx - \ux\w = 0, \qquad \ux = H\w.\vspace{-12pt}$$
The De Gregorio equation was later generalized by Okamoto et al. to include the real parameter $a$. The result is the Generalized Constantin-Lax-Majda equation (GCLM)\vspace{-12pt}
$$\wt + au\wx - \ux\w = 0, \qquad \ux = H\w.\vspace{-12pt}$$
The parameter $a$ determines the relative strength of the convection term. When $a=0$ the GCLM is equal to the CLM, and when $a=1$ it is equal to the De Gregorio equation. The CLM equation was proposed in 1985 and has been shown to exhibit finite-time blow-up. While the De Gregorio equation was proposed shortly after in 1989, questions still remain as to whether or not the equation permits finite-time blow-up. The goal of this analysis will be to examine the affect the parameter $a$ has on the finite-time blow-up of the GCLM equation. I will explore solutions of the GCLM for values of $a\in[-1,1]$.

\textbf{Abstract}

\noindent
The Constantine-Lax-Majda (CLM) equation was proposed as a one-dimensional model for the three-dimensional vorticity equation. The CLM equation was later expanded on by De Gregorio to include the convection term $u\wx$. The De Gregorio equation was generalized by Okamoto et al. to include the real parameter $a$ which determines the relative strength of the convection term. The result is the Generalized Constantin-Lax-Majda equation (GCLM). In this analysis, we examine the affect the parameter $a\in[-1,1]$, with increments of $0.1$, has on the finite-time blow-up of numerical solutions to the GCLM equation on the periodic domain $x\in[-\pi,\pi]$. 
\\~\\
The results of this analysis show that all values of $a\in[-1,0.8]$  resulted in the finite-time blow-up. In the case $a=1$ the solutions remain well-posed. The results for $a=0.9$ are inconclusive. This analysis also found that the time at which blow-up occurs can be approximated by an exponential function of $a$. However, due to a combination of numerical instability and insufficient resolution, we were unable to come to any definitive conclusions on the affect the parameter $a$ has on the rate of blow-up.

\section{Introduction}

The 3D Euler equations are a simplified form of the 3D Navier-Stokes equations which describe the flow of inviscid incompressible fluids. The 3D Euler equations are

\vspace{-30pt}

\begin{subequations}\label{eq:3D:Euler}
\begin{align}
\ut + u\cdot \nabla u + \nabla p &= 0, \\
\nabla \cdot u &= 0, \\
u(x,0) &= u_0(x),
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\vspace{-10pt}

\noindent
where $u$ is the velocity, $p$ is the scalar pressure, and $u_0$ is the initial condition. By substituting in the vorticity ($\w = \nabla \times u$) these equations can be re-expressed as the 3D vorticity equation

\vspace{-30pt}

\begin{subequations}\label{eq:3D:Vorticity}
\begin{align}
\wt + (u\nabla)\w &= (\w\nabla)u,\\
\w(x,0) &= \w_0(x),
\end{align}
\end{subequations}

\vspace{-10pt}

\noindent
The velocity is now computed from the vorticity with the Biot-Savart formula 

\vspace{-10pt}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:3D:Vel:Vort}
u(x,t) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\text{ P.V. }\int \frac{(x-y)}{|x-y|^3}\w(y,t)\,dy,
\end{equation}

\noindent
where P.V. indicates the integral is solved using the Cauchy Principal Value due to the singularity at $x=y$. A central question in fluid dynamics is whether or not finite-time singularities can form in fluid flows with smooth initial conditions \cite{constantin_simple_1985}. As first demonstrated by Beale, Kato, and Majda, singularities can form if and only if the maximum vorticity of the flow becomes infinite \cite{beale_remarks_1984}. The vorticity equation allows for the direct modelling of the vorticity and so is a very useful tool for studying singularity formation in the Euler equations. One of the principal reasons the question of singularity formation remains unanswered is the complexity of the problem, which arises from the Euler equations being a system of non-linear non-local PDEs \cite{elgindi_effects_2017}. These combined factors motivated the development of simplified 1D vorticity models which still possess many of the properties of 3D flows while being analytically and computationally simpler.

\subsection{The GCLM Equation}

The Constantin-Lax-Majda (CLM) model was the first 1D vorticity model for an inviscid incompressible fluid \cite{constantin_simple_1985}. The CLM equation is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:CLM}
\begin{align}
\wt &= H(\w)\w, \qquad x\in \R^1\\
\w(x,0) &= \w_0(x),
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $H(\w)$ is the Hilbert transform of the vorticity
\begin{equation}
H(\w) = \frac{1}{\pi}\text{ P.V. } \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{\w(y,t)}{(x-y)}\,dy.
\end{equation}
The velocity of the CLM model is defined as 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:CLM:Velocity}
u(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^x \w(y,t)\,dy.
\end{equation}
Constantin et al. proved that a solution to (\ref{eq:CLM}) will form finite-time singularities if and only if there exists a point $x_0$ where $w_0(x_0)=0$ and $H(\w_0)(x_0)>0$. A significant advantage of the CLM model was that its simplicity allowed for the derivation of an explicit solution to (\ref{eq:CLM}) \cite{constantin_simple_1985}. However, De Gregorio noted that while the CLM model does possess the vortex stretching properties of (\ref{eq:3D:Vorticity}), it does not include a convection term which limits its similarity to 3D flows. Due to the limitations of the CLM model, De Gregorio proposed a modified 1D model that improved upon the CLM model \cite{de_gregorio_one-dimensional_1990}. The De Gregorio equation is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:De:Gregorio}
\begin{align}
\wt + u\wx &= \ux\w, \qquad x\in\R^1, \\
u_x &= H(\w), \\
\w(x,0) &= \w_0(x).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The velocity is defined as
\begin{equation}
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{\pi}\text{ P.V. }\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\Omega(y,t)}{(x-y)}\,dy = H(\Omega)(x,t),
\end{equation}
where $\Omega(x,t)$ is the same formula as the velocity of the CLM model (\ref{eq:CLM:Velocity}). The De Gregorio equation includes the non-linear stretching term $u_x\w$, and the non-linear convection term $u\wx$. The convection term acts to move energy from small wave-numbers to higher wave-numbers and by doing so may act against blow-up. De Gregorio proposed that (\ref{eq:De:Gregorio}) is globally well posed, although he was not able to prove it \cite{de_gregorio_partial_1996}. The De Gregorio equation was later expanded on by Okamoto et al. to the Generalized Constantin-Lax-Majda (GCLM) model \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008}. The GCLM equation adds the real parameter $a$ which controls the relative strength of the convection term to the stretching term. In their analysis Okamoto et al. examined the GCLM model on the periodic domain $x\in[-\pi,\pi]$. The GCLM equation is
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:GCLM}
\begin{align}
\wt + au\wx - \ux\w &= 0, \qquad x\in[-\pi,\pi],\\
u_x &= H\w,\\
\w(x,0) &= \w_0(x).
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Because (\ref{eq:GCLM}) is defined on the periodic domain, the Hilbert transform is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hilbert:Transform:GCLM}
H\w(x,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\text{ P.V. }\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\w(y,t)\cot\lr\frac{x-y}{2}\rr\,dy.
\end{equation}
The velocity of the GCLM model is defined as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Velocity:GCLM}
u(x,t) = \frac{1}{\pi}\text{ P.V. }\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\w(y,t)\log\lv\sin\lr\frac{x-y}{2}\rr \rv \, dy.
\end{equation}
Okamoto et al. presented numerical evidence that the solution of (\ref{eq:GCLM}) exist globally when $a=1$, and proved the solution exists globally in the limit of $a\rightarrow\infty$ \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008}. When $a=1$, the GCLM model is equal to the De Gregorio equation, which has numerical evidence supporting the claim that it is globally well-posed. When $a=0$, the GCLM model is equal to the CLM model, which is known to form finite-time singularities. When $a<0$, the convection term no longer acts to suppress blow-up and instead contributes to it, and so blow-up is expected \cite{chen_singularity_2020}. 

\subsection{Initial Conditions}

Okamoto et al. noted that the solution of (\ref{eq:GCLM}) must satisfy
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\omega(t,x)\,dx = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} (-au\omega_x + u_x\omega)\,dx = (a+1)\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u_x\omega\,dx = (a+1)(H\omega,\omega),
\end{equation}
where  $(\cdot,\cdot)$ denotes the $L^2$ inner product. Therefore, the mean value of $\w(x,t)$ is independent of t. Based off of this, they use initial conditions in the following function spaces
\begin{subequations}\label{eq:Function:Spaces}
\begin{align}
L^2(S^1)/\R &= \lb f \big| f\in L^2(-\pi,\pi), \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)\,dx = 0\rb,\\
H^k(S^1)/\R &= \lb f \big| f=\sum_{n=1}^\infty(a_n\cos nx + b_n \sin nx),\sum_{n=1}^\infty (a_n^2 + b_n^2)n^{2k} < \infty \rb,
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where $k$ is a positive integer, $S^1$ denotes the unit circle in the plane, and $/\R$ implies that functions with zero mean are collected \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008}. The first initial condition they examined was
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Init:1}
w_{0}(x) = \sin(x)+0.1\sin(2x), \qquad x\in[-\pi, \pi], 
\end{equation}
which was computed numerically for the case $a=1$. We use this same initial condition as it allows us to verify our numerical methods by comparing our results for $a=1$ with those found in \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008}. The function (\ref{eq:Init:1}) has a very sparse Fourier spectrum, with only two non-zero modes. In order to gain a better understanding of the GCLM model, we must also investigate initial conditions with more complex behaviour. Therefore, we also consider the initial condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Init:2}
w_{0}(x) = e^{\sin(2x)} - e^{\cos(x)}, \qquad x\in[-\pi,\pi].
\end{equation}
The initial condition (\ref{eq:Init:2}) is analytic, periodic, and has a mean of zero. Therefore, it belongs to the (\ref{eq:Function:Spaces}) function spaces and can benefit from the pseudo-spectral methods used in this analysis.

\subsection{Blow-up Criteria}
In their analysis, Okamoto et al. prove the following Theorem, which is an analogue of the Beale–Kato–Majda theorem for the 3D Euler equations \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008}\cite{beale_remarks_1984}.

\begin{thm}\label{thm:Theorem:3.2}
Suppose that $\w_0\in H^1(S^1)/\R$, that the solution of (\ref{eq:GCLM}) exists in $[0,T)$ and that 
$$\int_0^T \|H\w(x,t)\|_{\infty}\,dt < \infty.$$
Then the solution exists in $0\leq t\leq T+\delta$ for some $\delta>0$.
\end{thm}
\noindent
In (\ref{eq:GCLM}), the derivative of the velocity is given by $u_x = H\w(x,t)$. Therefore, the first diagnostic quantity we track over time is 
\begin{equation}\label{eq:LINF}
q_1(t) = \int_0^t \|u_x(x,\tau)\|_{\infty}\,d\tau
\end{equation}
where $\|\cdot\|_{\infty}$ is the $L^{\infty}$-norm. Based on Theorem 1.1, Okamoto et al. derive the criteria that blow-up does not occurs if $\|\wx\|$ remains bounded, where $\|\cdot\|$ is the $L^2$-norm. In addition, they provide numerical evidence that for $a=1$ and all $T>0$
\begin{equation}
\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\wx(x,t)\| < \infty.
\end{equation}
Therefore, the second diagnostic quantity we track over time is
\begin{equation}
q_2(t) = \|\wx(x,t)\|.
\end{equation}
\noindent
These diagnostic quantities can be used to determine whether or not blow-up occurs because if $q_1$ and $q_2$ remain bounded over $0\leq t\leq T$, then blow-up does not occur. In Remark 5.1, Okamoto et al. note that the quantity $q_2$ is bounded by an exponential function of $q_1$ \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008}. Therefore, $q_2$ could become very large very quickly even if blow-up does not occur. Because of this, determining blow-up by setting an arbitrary finite threshold to these quantities will not provide useful insight whether or not blow-up of the solutions occurs. Instead, we base our determination of blow-up off of the method used in \cite{zhao_systematic_2023}. We assume the evolution of our diagnostic quantities, during blow-up, is determined by the relation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Diagnostic:Relation}
\frac{dq_i(t)}{dt} = C_i(t)q_i(t)^{\alpha_i(t)}, \qquad i=1,2.
\end{equation}
By plotting this function on a log-log graph, the rate of blow-up ($\alpha_i(t)$) is given by the slope of the plot. This slope does not necessarily remain constant. Therefore, the rate of blow-up is calculated over a window of time for each value of $a$. If $\alpha_i(t)>1$ and $C_i(t)>1$ for a sufficient period of time, then the solution will experience finite-time blow-up. This calculation is complicated by the fact that blow-up occurs at different times and has a different duration, for each value of $a$. Therefore, we must create a metric to evaluate the rate of blow-up over time without having to pick arbitrary windows for all 42 models examined in this report. Furthermore, to fully analyse the blow-up we compare and contrast the following two methods.
\\
The first method is to calculate the rate of blow-up by using uniform windows of time of size $t = 0.15$, which is at the end of the model and is divided into 12 equal blocks. The advantage of the block method is that the windows $\alpha_i(t)$ is calculated for are uniform for all values of $a$. The disadvantage is that these windows cover a large percentage of the blow-up for the some values of $a$ while covering a very small percentage for others. \subsection{Overview}

In this analysis, we compute numerical solutions to the GCLM equation with initial conditions (\ref{eq:Init:1}) and (\ref{eq:Init:2}) for $a\in[-1,1]$ with increments of 0.1. We base our numerical method on the pseudo-spectral method used in \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008} which is described in Section 2. The solutions are analysed using the block and percentage methods described above. In Section 3, we present numerical evidence for the well-posedness of the solution in the case of $a=1$, and for singularity formation in the solutions of the GCLM for $a\in[-1,0.8]$. The results for $a=0.9$ are also analysed but remain inconclusive. Finally, we summarize our analysis, its results, and shortcomings, as well as present potential future work in Section 4. Section 5 contains the derivations of the non-standard formulas used in this analysis.

\section{Numerical Methods}

To model (\ref{eq:GCLM}) numerically, we rearrange it to the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:GCLM:Numerical}
\wt = \ux\w - au\wx, \qquad x\in[-\pi,\pi].
\end{equation}
We compute the values of the four components, $\w$, $\wx$, $u$, and $\ux$ in Fourier space. These components are converted to physical space to calculate the non-linear convection term $\ux\w$ and the non-linear stretching term $u\wx$. We apply the 2/3 de-aliasing rule to the combination of the non-linear terms ($\ux\w - au\wx$). Time stepping is performed using the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method. As was done in \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008}, we also apply a spectral filter to reduce the growth of round-off errors. The complete methods for calculating these terms are described below. 
\\~\\
The evolution of the vorticity is computed for $a\in[-1,1]$ with increments of $0.1$. We use a maximum time-scale of $T=7$. However, most models are stopped early due to the vorticity becoming under-resolved or experiencing numerical instability. The models are computed with a resolution of $N=2^{14}$ and a time step size of $dt=1E-5$. 

\subsection{Physical and Fourier Representation}

The vorticity of (\ref{eq:GCLM:Numerical}) is represented in physical space on a grid of $N = 2^{14}$ equispaced points on the domain $x\in[-\pi,\pi]$. Because the domain is periodic, we must avoid double counting the grid point on the boundary. Therefore, we set $x_0=-\pi$ and $x_N=\pi-h$, where $h$ is the distance between points. The solution $\w$ is represented in Fourier space using a truncated Fourier series
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Vorticity:Truncated:Fourier}
w(x,t) = \sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}\wk(t)\exp(ikx).
\end{equation}
Transformation from physical to Fourier space, and vice versa, is performed using the standard Matlab 'fft' and 'ifft' functions respectively. Calculation of the derivative of the vorticity ($\wx$) is performed in Fourier space using the standard formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Vorticity:Deriv}
\wx(t) = \sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}(ik)\wk(t)\exp(ikx).
\end{equation} 
\noindent
The derivative of the velocity ($\ux$) is calculated using the Hilbert Transform (\ref{eq:Hilbert:Transform:GCLM}). However, due to the singularity at $x=y$, this equation must be solved using the Cauchy Principle value, which is difficult to compute numerically. Therefore, the derivative of the velocity is calculated in Fourier space using the formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Velocity:Deriv:Fourier}
u_x(x,t) = -\sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}i \sgn(k)\wk(t)e^{ikx},
\end{equation}
where sgn is the sign function. The derivation of this formula from (\ref{eq:Hilbert:Transform:GCLM}) is shown in Section 5.1. As shown in (\ref{eq:Velocity:GCLM}), the velocity can be computed from the vorticity as an integral in physical space. This integral also requires the use of the Cauchy Principle Value. Therefore, we compute the value of $u$ in Fourier space using the formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Velocity:Fourier}
u(x,t) = -\sum_{k=-N/2,k\neq 0}^{N/2 - 1}\frac{\sgn(k)\wk(t)\exp(ikx)}{k}.
\end{equation}
The derivation of this formula from (\ref{eq:Velocity:GCLM}) is shown in Section 5.2. We now have the four components needed to calculate the convection and stretching terms, with each component in Fourier space. Since we apply the 2/3 de-aliasing to the combined convection and stretching terms, we first apply zero-padding to each of the four terms then transform back to physical space. Once in physical space, we calculate the right hand side of (\ref{eq:GCLM:Numerical}) ($u_x\w - au\wx$). We convert the result back to Fourier space and and apply the 2/3 rule to prevent aliasing. While still in Fourier space, we apply a spectral filter by setting all Fourier modes below the threshold $1E-12$ to zero. Finally, we convert back to physical space.

\subsection{Time Stepping}

When modelling time dependent PDEs, it is best practice to use explicit time stepping methods for non-linear terms in order to reduce computation cost, and use implicit methods for linear term to improve stability \cite{protas_spectral_2025}. Both the convection term and stretching term of (\ref{eq:GCLM:Numerical}) are non-linear. Therefore, we perform time stepping using the explicit fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) method. The RK4 time stepping is performed with the formula
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RK4}
\omega^{(n+1)} = \omega^{(n)} + \frac{1}{6}(k_1 + 2k_2 + 2k_3 + k_4)
\end{equation}
where $\omega^{(n)}$ is the vorticity at iteration $n$ and the $k_i$ terms are calculated using
\begin{subequations}
\begin{align}
k_1 &= F(\omega^{(n)},t^{(n)})  \\
k_2 &= F(\omega^{(n)}+ dt \cdot k_1/2,t^{(n)} + dt/2)  \\
k_3 &= F(\omega^{(n)}+ dt \cdot k_2/2,t^{(n)}+ dt/2)  \\
k_4 &= F(\omega^{(n)}+ dt \cdot k_3,t^{(n)}+dt) 
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
where the function $F$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:RHS}
F(\omega^{(n)},t^{(n)}) = \ux\omega - au\wx,
\end{equation}
and so is calculated using the method described in Section 2.1.

\subsection{Complete Numerical Method}

Combining the methods described above, we use the following procedure for each time step

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Convert $\omega^{(n)}$ from Physical space to Fourier space using FFT.
  \item Calculate $\wx$, $ux$, and $u$ in Fourier space using (\ref{eq:Vorticity:Deriv}), (\ref{eq:Velocity:Deriv:Fourier}), and (\ref{eq:Velocity:Fourier}).
  \item Apply zero-padding and convert back to physical space.
  \item Calculate $u_x\w - au\wx$ in physical space.
  \item Convert back to Fourier space and apply 2/3 de-aliasing and the filter.
  \item Convert back to physical space.
  \item Repeat 1 - 6 for each of the $k_i$ terms in the RK4 method.
  \item Calculate $\w^{(n+1)}$ using (\ref{eq:RK4}).
\end{enumerate}
\noindent
This process is repeated until either the model reaches the maximum time of $T=7$, the model becomes under-resolved, or the model experiences numerical instability. To detect under-resolution and numerical instability, we check if the average value of the 100 largest Fourier modes in the vorticity are larger than a threshold value of 1E-10. If the average is above this threshold, the model is stopped. The plots in Figure 3 show examples of models that were stopped at the resolution limit (a) and due to numerical instability (b). The under-resolution and numerical instability were stopped early enough in all cases and so, even if the last step is inaccurate, the overall trend is still accurate and provide meaningful results. Additionally, we can consider the times the models were stopped to be close enough to the time at which blow-up occurs that we can still draw meaningful conclusions. This assertion will be supported with numerical evidence in Section 3.2.

\subsection{Diagnostic Quantities}

The $L^\infty$-norm and $L^2$-norm used for $q_1$ and $q_2$ are calculated using the Matlab 'norm' function. The integral in $q_1$ is calculated using the Matlab 'trapz' function, which uses the trapezoidal method to approximate the integral of a vector over a given domain with uniform spacing. For $q_1$ the derivative is 
\begin{equation}
\frac{dq_1}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int_0^t \|\ux(x,\tau)\|_{\infty} d\tau = \|\ux(x,t)\|_{\infty},
\end{equation}
\noindent
and so the log-log plots of $dq_1/dt$ vs $q_1$ is simplified to $\|\ux(x,t)\|_{\infty}$ vs $q_1$. The derivative of $q_2$ is simply the derivative of the $L^2$-norm over time, which is calculated using the forward difference method. To calculate the values of $\alpha_i(t)$, we perform fitting with the Matlab 'fit' function from the Curve Fitting Toolbox, which fits a function of the form $a*x^b$ to the selected data segments from the block and percentage methods described in Section 1.3.

\subsection{Results of $\boldsymbol a$ = 0.9}

Finally, we examine the case of $a=0.9$. In this case, the diagnostic quantities produce contradictory results. The rate of blow-up in Figure 9 for $a=0.9$ is below 1. The rate of blow-up in Figure 10 (a) shows that $\alpha_1(t)$ stays below 1 for the entire evolution and the plot in Figure 10 (b) show it is very slightly above 1. Overall, this would indicate that blow-up does not occur. However, Figures 11 and 12 show that for $a=0.9$, $\alpha_2(t)>1$ for the entire time window. This would indicate that blow-up does occur. This contradictory result may be due to the convection term being sufficiently large to initially suppress blow-up, but insufficient to stop blow-up in the longer term. If this is correct, then blow-up will occur, but at a time $t>T$. Alternatively, it may be that the stretching term initially dominates but in the long term is suppressed by the convection and so the solution remains globally well-posed. Unfortunately, the time-scale used in this analysis is insufficient to come to any definite conclusion on whether or not finite-time singularities can form in the GCLM equation for $a=0.9$.

\section{Conclusion}

The results of this analysis show that for $a=1$, the solutions of the GCLM equation are globally well-posed. For $a\in[-1,0.8]$, the rate of blow-up for the diagnostic quantity $q_1$ starts larger than 1 in all cases and accelerates over time. The rate of blow-up for $q_2$ also starts over 1 and stays over 1, but does decelerate and plateau over time. Therefore, these results demonstrate very strong numerical evidence for the formation of finite-time singularities in the solutions of the GCLM equation for $a\in[-1,0.8]$. The results show that blow-up occurs earlier as $a$ decreases and follows an exponential curve. In the case of $a=0.9$, the results are inconclusive. The diagnostic quantities used to determine blow-up produce contradictory results, with the $q_2$ indicating blow-up will occur and $q_1$ indicating blow-up will not occur. 
\\~\\
Based on the results of this analysis, we believe addition investigation is worth pursuing. In particular, repeating this analysis with increased resolution and a greater diversity of initial conditions. 

\section{Appendix}
\subsection{Derivation of Hilbert Transform in Fourier space}
The Hilbert transform is defined as
$$H\w(x,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\w(y,t)\cot\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)\,dy.$$
The solution $\w(y,t)$ is represented as the truncated Fourier series
$$\w(y,t) = \sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}\wk(t)\exp(iky).$$
Substituting this into the Hilbert transform
$$H\w(x,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}\wk(t)\exp(iky)\cot\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)\,dy.$$
We can swap the integral and sum to obtain
$$H\w(x,t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}\wk(t) \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \exp(iky)\cot\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right)\,dy.$$
The integral has a singularity at $x=y$ and so must be evaluated using the Cauchy Principle Value. The Cauchy Principle values of this equation is the well known result used in \cite{hideki_numerical_2006} and \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008},
$$H\w(x,t) = -\sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}i \sgn(k)\wk(t)e^{ikx}$$
where $\sgn$ is the sign function 
$$\sgn(k) = \begin{cases}
  1, & \text{if } k > 0 \\
  0, & \text{if } k = 0 \\
  -1 & \text{if } k < 0.
\end{cases}$$

\subsection{Derivation of the Velocity in Fourier space}

The velocity field is defined as
$$u(x,t) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\w(y,t)\log\left| \sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) \right|\,dy.$$
The solution $\w(y,t)$ is represented as the truncated Fourier series
$$\w(y,t) = \sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}\w_k(t)\exp(iky).$$
Substituting this into the velocity equation
$$u(t,x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} \sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}\wk(t)\exp(iky)\log\left| \sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) \right|\,dy.$$
We can swap the integral and sum to obtain
$$u(t,x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \sum_{k=-N/2}^{N/2 - 1}\wk(t) \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\exp(iky)\log\left| \sin\left(\frac{x-y}{2}\right) \right|\,dy.$$
The integral has a singularity at $x=y$ and so must be evaluated using the Cauchy Principle Value. The Cauchy Principle values of this equation is
$$u(t,x) = -4\ln(2)w_0(t) - \sum_{k=-N/2,k\neq 0}^{N/2 - 1}\frac{\sgn(k)\wk(t) \exp(ikx)}{k}.$$
For functions $w(x,t)$ with a mean of zero over the domain, the Fourier coefficient $\hat{w}_0(t)$ is zero. Since the mean of the solution is constant over time, and we use initial conditions with a mean of zero, we can simplify the velocity equation to that used in \cite{hideki_numerical_2006} and \cite{okamoto_generalization_2008},
$$u(t,x) = -\sum_{k=-N/2,k\neq 0}^{N/2 - 1}\frac{\sgn(k)\wk(t) \exp(ikx)}{k}$$
where $\sgn$ is the sign function 
$$\sgn(k) = \begin{cases}
  1, & \text{if } k > 0 \\
  0, & \text{if } k = 0 \\
  -1 & \text{if } k < 0.
\end{cases}$$
